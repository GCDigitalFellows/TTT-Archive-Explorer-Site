A reading of the archive · 2012–2026

Digital scholarship is a collective practice

The Tagging the Tower archives portray digital scholarship as learning, making, maintaining and exercising judgment together—and the GC Digital Fellows as people who sustain that practice across disciplines and changing technologies.

This is an interpretive reading of selected posts, supported by 19 passages checked against the supplied WordPress export. How these examples were selected—and what changed after review

The initial 14 examples were chosen purposively to illustrate the six proposed themes, not sampled randomly or coded as a representative subset. The two simulated AI reviewers prompted a coverage check: the essay had omitted 2018–2022, which contains 98 published local/unflagged records.

For this revision, Codex screened the titles of those 98 records and read five additional posts in full, selecting one from each missing year for its potential to complicate the argument: earlier AI skepticism, access and governance, pandemic-era collaboration, code as a way of thinking, and the politics of repair. The new passages qualify the goal-first, timeless-community and recent-AI narratives. This targeted review does not claim exhaustive coverage, independent human coding, thematic saturation or a systematic search for every dissenting view. Remaining zero years are visible below.

Theme 01

Scholarly purpose and technical choices shape each other

The archive repeatedly starts with research problems, available time, skills and support. Digital scholarship emerges as choosing and adapting tools to scholarly purposes, rather than accumulating technical novelty. Yet Jessie McCormack’s 2021 reflection complicates a purely goal-first account: learning code can itself change how a researcher thinks about language and possible questions.

Theme 04

Ethics operates inside the method

Maggie Schreiner’s metadata discussion, Parisa Setayesh’s account of bounded commons and Silvia Rivera Alfaro’s discussion of Humanidades Digitales connect technical decisions to description, consent, language and community. Openness requires attention to context and care; access alone does not settle every ethical question. Kristen Hackett’s 2019 community-wireless essay already treats access as participation in governance and maintenance, extending the argument beyond campus tools. The political and technical examples are historical source claims, not independently reverified current conditions.

Theme 05

AI prompts different judgments across the archive

Eunah Cho questions whether more activity produces better judgment. Nicole Walker describes an AI-assisted response to a teaching problem. Ian G. Williams situates AI literacy within institutions, labor and mutual support. Together these posts suggest conditional experimentation and critical judgment, rather than a single institutional position on AI. Walker’s reported benefits are observations, not controlled evidence of a causal effect. Rachel Rakov’s 2018 critique of purported bot-written memes shows that scrutiny of AI claims predates the recent publishing surge. Her statements about what contemporary neural networks could do belong to 2018 and must not be read as current technical guidance.

What this reading can—and cannot—establish

The archive records how contributors represent digital scholarship through tutorials, announcements, reflections and selected participant testimony. It supports an account of recurring values and practices, but does not establish program-wide effectiveness, every participant’s experience or causal differences between Fellow cohorts. A post’s academic year is publication context; it does not automatically establish its writer’s cohort membership. See the index and cohort views for the separate attribution and roster evidence.
