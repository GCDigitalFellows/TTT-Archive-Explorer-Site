## Possible arguments to explore, with passages to read

These are editorial starting points, not measured findings about the whole archive:

- **An institute can change a participant’s relationship to learning.** Maren Rothfritz, [Attending the DRI is like Savoring a Multi-Course Coding Meal](https://gcdi.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2023/05/01/attending-the-dri-is-like-savoring-a-multi-course-coding-meal/). Participation is not itself evidence of a fellowship appointment.
- **Convening can be scholarly work.** [Reflections on the inaugural “Conversations in Digital Scholarship”](https://gcdi.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2025/05/23/reflections-on-the-inaugural-conversations-in-digital-scholarship/), credited to Tuka Al-Sahlani, Silvia Rivera Alfaro, Parisa Setayesh, Maggie Schreiner, Anna Corbett, Zachary Lloyd, Cen Liu, Maura McCreight, Cortnie Belser, Peyton Cordero and Chen Zhou. Read the different reflections rather than treating eleven credits as eleven votes for one argument.
- **Openness involves boundaries and care.** Parisa Setayesh, [The Walled Garden: Care and Containment in AI Research](https://gcdi.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2025/10/17/the-walled-garden-care-and-containment-in-ai-research/).
- **Activity and judgment can diverge.** Eunah Cho, [When AI Makes Us More Active, We Risk Being Less Wise](https://gcdi.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2026/04/10/when-ai-makes-us-more-active-we-risk-being-less-wise/).
- **Infrastructure affects who can begin.** Shokraan Rahiminezhad, [GeoLibre: Open-Source GIS in Your Browser](https://gcdi.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2026/11/09/geolibre-open-source-gis-in-your-browser/).

The first three examples have source-supported credits in the saved corpus; the latter three use declared author metadata. Source texts retain their separate rights. This list is not a new authorship audit.

## Archive introduction: prompts, not ghostwritten experience

- What was Tagging The Tower written for, and under what institutional and educational conditions?
- What does the experience of doctoral students contribute that a tool manual or annual report might leave out? Treat this comparison as your argument, not a measured uniqueness claim.
- What changes across the archive, and what persists: uncertainty, maintenance, mutual support, technical constraints, judgment?
- Which disagreements or absences complicate the story you want to tell?
- What can a reader do here: read a voice, follow a question, search a problem, inspect a pattern, return to a source?

## Personal account of building with AI

Possible sequence: your starting question → an encounter with the tool → a useful result → an error or disagreement → a revision → what remains unresolved.

You might discuss byline corrections, the effects of corpus cleaning, the challenge of making charts readable, or the accessibility feedback that prompted this redesign. Refer to public-safe project records rather than reproducing raw chats or precise activity traces. Write what you experienced and checked; distinguish it from what the software computed or the AI proposed.

Questions to keep visible: What did you delegate? What did you decide? What did you verify yourself? Which outputs remain provisional? How did other people’s feedback change the work? What responsibility remains with the people presenting the archive?
