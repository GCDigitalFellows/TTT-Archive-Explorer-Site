Living and learning with digital transformation

Tagging The Tower is a blog about digital scholarship, managed and published by GC Digital Initiatives: the tools people use, the questions they ask, and how they learn and work together in community.

This interactive website brings together a digital anthology of writing by GC Digital Fellows and other contributors, including students, institute participants and guests. It offers tutorials, reflections and accounts of scholarly practice across changing technical and institutional conditions, spanning over 14 years and containing 423 published articles. The website is designed to facilitate close and distant reading of the texts - that is human reading of individual posts and passages, and computational analysis of the corpus as a whole, and through selections of sections and time periods. This portion includes interactive visualizations that help raise questions about the meanings that surface across Tagging The Tower over time.

An introduction to the archive

Ian G. Williams · Introduction (Draft)

In January 2022, I participated in the Graduate Center Digital Research Institute, an annual intensive coding skills and digital scholarship led by GC Digital Fellows. At that time, I was taking a statistics course where we worked in the open-sourced programming language R, in a "cookbook" fashion where we followed recipes to clean, inspect, and analyze data with no attention paid to the historical, cultural, and epistemic assumptions build into our application of these technologies. DRI was a breath of fresh air, and one I found quite invigoration. That spring, I was invited that to write a post for Tagging The Tower. A first-year PhD student, I was learning much about the rhythms of academia, and writing. The blog post offered me a chance to write and revise some fleeting thoughts, trying to organize a meaningful learning experience that didn't fit in with my class assignments, teaching lessons, or research assistant duties. I have since written several other posts: one on my experience as a Program Social Media Fellow, one about the concept of AI literacy in relation to a Data & Society Research Institute Workshop on the topic, and two reflecting this fall on my experience participating in CityCamp NYC, a public interest technology unconference.

I enjoy this form of writing, which can often be personal, reflective, and conversational - with a sprinkling of theory and academic references. I am an older Millenial who grew up into the Internet, and particpated in early forms of chatrooms, blogging, and social media. I had accounds on AOL Instant Messenger, MySpace, and LiveJournal. Although it might seem a bit passé today, academic blogging (originally, web logging)is a form of public scholarship that predates much of social media, and the current form of newsletter platforms that serve, in many ways, as elaborate forms of blogging (and at times, podcasting and video blogging). Tagging The Tower, hosted through the CUNY Academic Commons, is a form of scholarly blogging shaped by its emergence in the era of WordPress and open-sourced academic publishing.

Tagging The Tower on GCDI

When preparing for a Digital Fellows event this fall at the NYC Public Interest Technology Pop Up, I wondered where I might find wisdom and records of what the Digital Fellows do, and how they thought, as a collective entity over time, about their work? The Digital Fellows has been extremely meaningful to my journey and I've tried to weave their work into civic and public interest technology spaces before, such as when I organized a panel at the NYC School of Data in 2024 that featured several friends who were Digital Fellows at that time, presenting on an eccentric melange of projects that showed how creative, thoughtful, and interdisciplinary the program is. I started reading through Tagging The Tower posts on GC Digital Initiatives' CUNY Academic Commons page, and felt limited by the format in which it was organized within WordPress, and thought this might be a good excuse to experiment with porting and transposing the posts to another medium that made it easier to sort through, and could be enhanced by computational tools and analyses. Short on time, curious about how it might play out, and not operating in an environment where I felt normrative pressures to contain and constrain my use of artificial intelligence (AI), I decided build an interactive website through vibe-coding, or the practice of giving natural language instructions to a large language model (LLM) artificial intelligence (AI) system. I chose a frontier model in part because I had access - despite my skepticism and constant critique of Big AI, I've held onto a paid ChatGPT account since experimenting in 2023 with AI image creation - and in part because I wanted to experience and test the capabilities of it in ways I had not before. Creating for the PIT Lab, I wanted to explore this process of "building" with AI. I wanted to see what it would do, and how I would steer the direction of the project, and iterate over time.

The result is this website, and its several iterations nested within the repository. It contains statistical analysis of the blog posts, and several visualizations of the data. It also contains some information about the work processes behind it, including the rhythms and times in which I worked on it in concert with the AI tool, and screenshots of earlier versions. It is still quite messy and a work-in-progress. My aim is to provoke discussion about the blog, and the tools used to create this website. I make no generalized claims or arguments about AI use for these tools, but I do situate myself within the work and the profound sense of ambivalence and mixed emotions that constantly surface when I work with AI tools.

How this website was made

The website combines an archive, computational analysis, and human decisions about how to read and present the results.

My experience of building this website with an AI tool

Ian G. Williams · Human-authored narrative

Prior to this project, I used an AI tool (Gemini CLI) to build a much simpler website while working as a Fellow with the CUNY AI Lab in Spring 2026. In that case, it was a timer website that I built out from a semi-working template. I called it Frankentimer, and I experimented with various functions and style variations. That was a contained and bound project, a website with an existing purpose and set of parameters. In this case, I took a data set - the corpus of Tagging The Tower in an XML file - and, using a paid chatGPT account, had it build a website from the data.

This was quite different from my previous experiences with data analysis and web development, where cleaning and inspecting data is a fundamental part of the process that informs design and prototyping choices. In this case, I tried naively feeding the data in and seeing what truth effects it produced - how it was potentially workable and knowable. My process was iterative and also included a lot of experimentation, tear-downs, and rebuilds. I had working code I hadn't written or inspected, and so I wasn't quite sure how to understand it or question what it produced.

Design evolution

The first three screenshots show parallel layout proposals. Later screenshots show changes to the interface and charts.

1. Read the sources

Published posts supply the writing. Posting accounts, article bylines and fellowship records are different kinds of evidence.

2. Compare patterns

Saved word counts and MALLET topic models offer ways to compare texts. Cleaning choices and corpus boundaries shape those patterns.

3. Return to people and passages

Read the original posts, inspect attribution and question the interpretation. A model result is not the authors’ collective agreement.
